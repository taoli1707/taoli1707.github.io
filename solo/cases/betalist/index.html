Overview

BetaList features pre-launch and newly launched startups to an audience of early adopters. Marc Köhlbrugge started it in December 2010 on Tumblr. After 10 years it had featured 15,000+ startups, reviewed 70,000+ submissions, welcomed 100,000+ registered users and made almost $1M in total revenue. It makes money from advertising and sponsorships and by charging founders to skip the free review queue.

Origin

In late 2010 Köhlbrugge and friends were building Openmargin, an iPad app, and struggling to get beta sign-ups. Over a weekend he built BetaLi.st, a modified Tumblr theme collecting 'coming soon' pages, and pitched it to TechCrunch, hoping to redirect the coverage to his own app. TechCrunch wrote about it in December 2010. He didn't plan to monetize it until companies started asking how to get featured.

How it makes money

(1) Advertising: he started paid slots at $50/week and kept doubling the price until buyers said no, reaching $1,500/week by 2020. The advertise page now lists a $4,999/month sponsorship and self-serve 'Boost' placements at $99/week or $199/month. (2) Expedited review: he first let founders skip the queue for free as a favor, then charged $15, and kept raising it to $199 by 2020.

What drove growth

  • A free submission queue with a paid fast lane. The backlog itself creates demand for the paid option.
  • Early press: a December 2010 TechCrunch article, and later Marc's 2014 post 'How I tricked TechCrunch into writing about my startup', which he says drove even more visits than the original article.
  • Each featured startup shares its listing, which brings in more founders and early-adopter subscribers.
  • He kept testing prices on ad slots and expedited reviews, raising them until demand dropped.

Moat & risks

What protects it

More than a decade of brand recognition in the startup-launch niche, an email and user base of early adopters, and domain authority that makes a listing worth paying for.

What could hurt it

Launch directories are easy to start and there are hundreds now. Product Hunt dominates attention, and a directory's value falls if its audience stops engaging.

Lessons you can reuse

  1. A free queue plus a paid skip-the-line option is a simple, scalable way for a directory to make money.
  2. Price ads by doubling until customers say no. His started at $50/week and reached $1,500/week.
  3. Start with no-code (a Tumblr theme) and rebuild only after demand shows up.
  4. Delegating daily operations freed him to start other products (WIP, startup.jobs) while BetaList kept earning.

01

What to build

The number of agent tools and MCP servers is growing fast, and discovery is fragmented across GitHub and X. Builders will pay for fast review and a trustworthy 'tested' badge.

02

Market

Buyers are developers and small AI-tool startups who want users; reach them through X, Hacker News, Reddit and a weekly newsletter. Competitors include Smithery, mcp.so, Glama's MCP directory, the official MCP registry and general launch sites like Product Hunt.

03

30-day path to a first paying customer

  1. Scrape GitHub for MCP server repos and seed 300 listings with auto-extracted metadata
  2. Run each through a scripted install test and publish pass/fail
  3. Add a free submit form with a 3-4 week queue and a $49 'review in 48h' option via Stripe
  4. Post the weekly 'new tools' list on X and in AI builder communities to grow the audience
04

Tech stack

  • Next.js
  • Postgres
  • GitHub API
  • Docker (sandboxed install tests)
  • Stripe
  • Resend (newsletter)
05

Revenue model

Free listings with a slow queue, a $49 one-time expedited review with a 'tested' badge, and $99/week featured placements once the newsletter has a few thousand subscribers.

This plan is our idea, not the founder's, and the market notes are our judgment. We haven't validated the demand: talk to 10 potential buyers before you write code.
