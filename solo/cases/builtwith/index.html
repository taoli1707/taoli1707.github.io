Overview

BuiltWith crawls the web, detects which technologies each site uses (CMS, analytics, ecommerce platform, CDN and so on), and sells searchable lists of those sites. The main buyers are sales and marketing teams who want leads filtered by technology. The ~$14M/yr figure is repeated widely, most recently in 2022-2024 coverage, but BuiltWith has never published audited financials.

Origin

In 2007 Gary Brewer was going to Sydney startup meetups and viewing the source of startup sites to see what they were built with. No tool automated that, so he built one and launched it in July 2007. He worked on it evenings for about four years while holding a full-time job. Coverage says it was already making about $40,000 a month while it was still a side project.

How it makes money

Monthly subscriptions to the full dataset: Basic $295/mo, Pro $495/mo, Team $995/mo, with a discount for annual billing. A free tier allows a handful of lookups a day. In 2015 Brewer told Startup Daily it had 'a few thousand' paying users, split about 40% Basic, 40% Pro and 20% Enterprise.

What drove growth

  • A free single-site lookup ('what is this site built with?') brings in very large organic traffic. Coverage cites about 2M monthly page views, all organic.
  • Users started asking for lists, such as Australian sites on a CDN or stores running Magento. Answering those requests turned a curiosity tool into a lead-generation product.
  • The crawl data compounds. Historical records of when a site added or dropped a technology are hard for a newcomer to recreate.
  • Pricing is a few high tiers aimed at B2B sales teams who can easily justify $300-1,000 a month, not at many low-price consumers.

Moat & risks

What protects it

More than 15 years of crawl history and technology-detection rules, plus the organic search traffic a lookup tool with that much history earns. A new competitor can detect technologies today but cannot go back and create the historical adoption and churn data.

What could hurt it

Competitors (Wappalyzer, SimilarTech, HG Insights) and cheap in-house detection with headless browsers push prices down. Lead-list data also depends on privacy and scraping rules staying roughly where they are.

Lessons you can reuse

  1. A free lookup tool can serve as the SEO engine, while the paid product is the same data sold in bulk.
  2. Watch what power users ask for. BuiltWith's lead-list business came from people emailing to ask for exports.
  3. Price B2B data by the value of the leads it produces (hundreds of dollars a month), not by what it costs to build.
  4. Data that builds up over time, such as history and change logs, is a stronger moat than a one-time scrape.

Idea to build next

An 'AI stack' detector that shows which LLM providers, vector databases and agent frameworks companies use, drawn from JS bundles, DNS/CNAMEs, status-page vendors and job posts, and sold as lead lists to AI tooling vendors.

Why now: BuiltWith-style detectors are built around front-end technology fingerprints. Most AI adoption happens on the backend and is only visible through indirect signals such as job posts, subprocessor lists and SDK traces.

30-day path to a first paying customer

  1. Pick 30 signals, such as OpenAI/Anthropic SDK strings in public JS, Pinecone/Weaviate hostnames, and 'LangChain' in job posts
  2. Crawl 50k B2B SaaS domains plus their careers and subprocessor pages, and store results with timestamps
  3. Publish a free 'what AI does X use?' lookup page per company for SEO
  4. Sell a $199/mo CSV export filtered by signal to 3-5 AI infrastructure startups, found through LinkedIn outreach to their growth leads

This is our idea, not the founder's. We haven't validated the demand: talk to 10 potential buyers before you write code.
