Overview

Justin Welsh is a former SaaS sales executive who sells self-paced courses (The LinkedIn Operating System, The Content Operating System, The Creator MBA) to an audience built on LinkedIn and X, funneled through a weekly newsletter. He reported $4.15M+ in 2024 revenue at about 86% margins, and in June 2025 wrote that the business had passed $10M in total revenue with no employees.

Origin

Welsh was SVP of Sales at healthcare SaaS company PatientPop and left after burning out in 2019. He started posting daily on LinkedIn about sales and growth and did some consulting. In April 2020 he put a short $50 course, The LinkedIn Playbook, on Gumroad and made $10,482 in its first month. He rebuilt it as The LinkedIn Operating System, pre-selling it in July 2021 about 30 days before it went live.

How it makes money

Mostly one-time-purchase digital courses sold from his own site; The LinkedIn Operating System lists at $200. Other streams he has disclosed: consulting, newsletter sponsorships (two slots per issue at $2,500 each), subscriptions such as The Monthly Templates, and paid communities (an early one he closed, and the newer Unsubscribe membership). The free Saturday newsletter (started January 2022) is the main funnel into the courses and The Creator MBA.

What drove growth

  • Posted on LinkedIn and later X daily for years, repurposing the same ideas across both platforms; no paid ads
  • Tested demand with a cheap $50 course, then rebuilt it and raised the price 3-4x; the second version grossed $186K in 3 months
  • Asks course buyers to join an affiliate program; he reports 3,247 affiliates generating $600K in revenue
  • Launched The Saturday Solopreneur newsletter in January 2022; reported roughly 77K subscribers after one year and 185K by mid-2025
  • Used an 18-week personalized launch plan for The Creator MBA, which sold $1.6M in six days in January 2024

Moat & risks

What protects it

A very large personal following on LinkedIn and X plus a newsletter list, both tied to his personal story. Buyers are paying for his specific system and credibility, which a copycat course can't easily replicate.

What could hurt it

Revenue depends on one person's attention and on platform algorithms (LinkedIn, X). The 'how to grow an audience' course market is crowded and prone to saturation.

Lessons you can reuse

  1. Pre-sell a course before building it; a 30-day pre-sale gives both validation and a deadline
  2. Build the audience on one or two platforms with daily posting before expecting product sales
  3. Use a free newsletter as the owned channel that turns social followers into buyers
  4. Keep costs low (no staff, off-the-shelf tools) so most revenue becomes profit
  5. Productize your prior career skill (sales, growth) rather than a topic you are new to

A course and newsletter teaching licensed tradespeople (electricians, HVAC techs) to leave employment and run a one-van business, built from one tradesperson's own numbers

01

What to build

Solopreneur education is aimed almost entirely at knowledge workers; trades have high demand and few people teaching the business side in plain language.

02

Market

Buyers are licensed electricians, plumbers and HVAC techs thinking about going solo, reached through Facebook trade groups, YouTube and trade subreddits. Competitors are trade-business coaches and programs such as Tommy Mello's Home Service Expert and ServiceTitan's free content.

03

30-day path to a first paying customer

  1. Post daily on LinkedIn/Facebook trade groups about your own pricing, quoting and client numbers
  2. Collect emails with a free quoting spreadsheet
  3. Pre-sell a $99 'first 90 days solo' course to the list with a 30-day delivery date
  4. Deliver it as 6-8 recorded screen shares plus templates
04

Tech stack

  • Kit (ConvertKit)
  • Teachable or Podia
  • Stripe
  • Loom
  • Google Sheets templates
05

Revenue model

Start with a free quoting spreadsheet as the lead magnet, a $99 pre-sold course, and a $29/mo templates-and-Q&A membership once 50 students have finished it.

This plan is our idea, not the founder's, and the market notes are our judgment. We haven't validated the demand: talk to 10 potential buyers before you write code.
