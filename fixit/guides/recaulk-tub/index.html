How to Re-Caulk a Bathtub or Shower (and Make It Last)

Re-caulk a tub or shower so it lasts: strip every bit of old caulk, kill the mildew, dry it, fill the tub, then run one bead of 100% silicone. About 2 hours.

Caulk fails for four reasons: mildew grew on it, it was applied over old caulk, the joint was damp, or the tub moves when someone steps in and the bead tears. The fix for all four is in the method: strip everything, clean and dry, and, the trick almost nobody knows, fill the tub with water before you caulk so the joint is at its widest while the silicone cures. A bead applied to an empty tub stretches every time you shower.

Use 100% silicone for the tub joint. It stays flexible and mildew-resistant for years. Acrylic caulks are easier to work with but harden and crack in a wet joint. The tube says which it is.

⚠️ Before you start
  • Ventilate. Silicone caulk and caulk remover fumes are strong; open the window and run the fan while you work and while it cures.
  • Utility blades in a wet tub are a slip hazard. Work with the tub dry, wear shoes with grip, and retract the blade whenever you're not cutting.
  • Diluted bleach (1 part to 10 water) kills mildew. Never mix it with anything else, and rinse well before the alcohol wipe.

Step by step

0 of 9 steps

  1. Cut along both edges of the old bead with the utility knife held nearly flat, then pull the ribbon out with the removal tool. Scrape what's left with the plastic razor. Old silicone leaves a clear film that new caulk won't stick to: rub the joint with a dry finger, and if it feels slick, apply remover gel, wait, and scrape again. Every trace has to go.

    Careful: New silicone does not bond to old silicone. Incomplete removal is the number one reason a fresh bead peels in a month.

  2. Spray the joint with mildew cleaner or the bleach solution, wait ten minutes, scrub with an old toothbrush, and rinse. Then wipe the joint with alcohol on a rag to remove soap film and the last of the moisture.

  3. No showers for 12–24 hours before caulking. Run the hair dryer along the whole joint just before you start. Any moisture in the joint means a weak bond and mildew under the bead.

  4. Fill the tub to the overflow with water and leave it full until the caulk has cured. The weight opens the joint to its widest, so the cured bead is never stretched in use. For stall showers there's nothing to fill; just keep everyone out for 24 hours.

  5. Optional but worth it for a professional line: run painter's tape along the tub and along the wall, each about 1/8 in from the joint. Press the edges down.

  6. Cut the nozzle at 45° so the opening is a little smaller than the gap. Puncture the inner seal with the gun's rod or a long nail. Load the tube and squeeze until caulk just reaches the tip.

  7. Hold the gun at 45° and pull it along the joint in one continuous motion at a steady speed, keeping the nozzle in the corner so the caulk fills the joint instead of sitting on top. Do one side of the tub at a time, corners included, without stopping mid-run. Release the pressure before you lift off.

  8. Within five minutes, before it skins: wet a finger with soapy water (or use the tool) and draw it along the bead in one smooth pass with light pressure, pushing caulk into the joint and leaving a slight concave. Wipe your finger on a paper towel between passes. Pull the tape immediately, at a 45° angle away from the bead, and lightly smooth any tiny edge the tape left.

    Tip: If a section goes wrong, wipe it all out with alcohol right away and redo that section. Don't try to fix it after it skins.

  9. Leave it 24 hours before water touches it (fast-cure products say 3–12 hours on the tube; believe the tube). Then drain the tub. Wipe the bead dry after showers for the first week or two while the mildew resistance is fresh, and the bead stays white for years.

Still not working?

The new bead is peeling

Residue or moisture under it. Strip it (fresh silicone comes off easily), do the alcohol wipe and the hair dryer, and redo.

Mildew came back in months

Usually ventilation. Run the fan for 20 minutes after every shower, and squeegee the walls. Make sure the tube said “mildew-resistant” and “100% silicone.”

The gap is huge and the caulk sags

Gaps over 1/4 in need backer rod pushed into the joint first, then caulk over it. Two thin passes beat one thick one.

The bead is lumpy

Nozzle cut too big, or stopping and starting. Strip it while fresh and re-run at a steady speed. A dripless gun helps more than any technique.

Call a pro if…

  • The wall or tile is soft, spongy, or moves when you press it. Water has been getting behind it and there's likely rot; the wall needs opening.
  • Caulk keeps failing within months in the same spot: the tub is flexing (unsupported underneath) or the wall is moving.
  • Grout is failing across whole walls, not just at the corner joints. Regrouting is a different, larger job.

Questions people ask

Silicone or acrylic?

Tub, shower, sink rim: 100% silicone. Baseboards, trim, anything you'll paint: acrylic (silicone can't be painted).

How often should caulk be redone?

When it cracks, pulls away, or goes black under the surface. Well-applied silicone lasts 5–10 years; a bad job lasts 6 months.

Can I just caulk over the old stuff?

No. It will peel, and you'll have twice as much to remove next time.

Why fill the tub?

A tub full of water and a person weighs several hundred pounds and sinks slightly on its supports, opening the joint. Caulk cured in the open position compresses in use instead of stretching. Stretching is what tears it.
