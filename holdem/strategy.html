Everything below runs in this tab. No hand histories are uploaded and nothing is sent anywhere — the whole system is about forty kilobytes of JavaScript and a precomputed equity table.

How it works

One agent that answers "what should I do here?" end to end has to compress everything it knows into a single number, and once it does, you can no longer see why it wants to fold. That is fine for a bot and useless for learning. So this is nine agents instead.

Each specialist gets the same situation and looks at it through exactly one lens. None of them may return an action. They return a distribution over the legal actions plus a confidence, and they must show the numbers they used. The chair pools those votes, weighted by each agent's confidence and by how much authority that lens has in this particular spot — the solver's word is close to final at 8 big blinds and worth precisely nothing at 100.

Why they are allowed to abstain

The first version of this averaged nine opinions and it had a specific, embarrassing failure: it never bluffed. Hold seven-six on an ace-king board against someone who folds three times in four, and the equity agent reported two percent, the texture agent reported no showdown value, the stack agent reported nothing worth protecting — and all three voted to check. They were each correct about their own lens and collectively wrong, because none of those lenses can see fold equity at all.

So agents now abstain. When the hand has no showdown value and nobody has bet, the equity agent's headline reads "too little to bet for value, so this is a bluff-or-give-up decision and equity cannot settle it" — and it votes 50/50 at a confidence of 0.15 instead of vetoing. The question passes to the agent that can answer it. That single change is the difference between a system that folds every profitable bluff and one that finds them.

Why a close vote is not a frequency

The chair's output looks like a mixed strategy and it is tempting to play it as one. That is wrong, and it is wrong by about 250 big blinds per hundred hands — which is how it was found.

The playing agent originally sampled its action from the distribution: fold 25% of the time when fold held a quarter of the weight. Against the engine's own much simpler equity bot it lost at −283 ± 125 bb/100. Taking the top action instead, changing nothing else, brought the same matchup to −32 ± 100 — inside its own interval.

The reason is that a vote share measures how much the nine lenses agree, not how often an action is correct. A quarter of the weight on folding means a quarter of the argument leaned that way; it does not mean folding is right one hand in four. Real mixing does exist in poker, and there is exactly one place on this page where the frequencies are genuine equilibrium frequencies rather than opinion polls: the solver's push/fold output, which is computed rather than voted on.

So a close vote is a flag, not a prescription. It marks a spot whose answer turns on the read you supplied — which is a reason to go and check the read, and precisely what the adversary agent is there to help with.

Preflop: the part that is actually solved

Heads-up jam-or-fold is one of the very few poker decisions with a real answer. The tree is two nodes deep — the small blind shoves or folds, the big blind calls or folds — which is small enough to solve outright rather than estimate. The solver in this engine reaches an equilibrium exploitable for well under — big blinds per hand, which is zero for any purpose you have.

So the first thing to know about strategy is the depth at which each hand stops being a jam. Brighter means it stays a jam deeper; hover any cell for the number.

    Where the council has nothing useful to say

    That is not a bug, and it is not quite wrong either. Facing half a big blind into a pot of one and a half, the price is 25% — and there is no starting hand in the deck with less than 25% equity against a range that wide. Every lens the council owns says the call is correct, and every lens is right. The output is simply useless, because the real question at a hundred big blinds is not "does this call show a profit against the immediate price" but "what does this hand do for the range I will be playing on three future streets", and none of the nine lenses model future streets at all.

    So the grid was removed rather than shipped. A system that returns one action for every hand in the deck has told you nothing, and putting colour on it would only have hidden that. The short-stack grid above stays because there the tree really does end after one decision — which is the same reason it can be solved at all.

    Read the last column as a warning rather than an instruction. It is the frequency below which any two cards can bet at you profitably — not a licence to call with anything to reach it. Defend with the hands that have the most equity and the best blockers, and if you cannot find enough of them, your range arrived on this street too weak.

    The two rows that matter

    The nit, a hundred big blinds deep. This is the matchup the engine's older, simpler equity bot could not win: it scored +7 ± 15 against an opponent folding three times in four — no edge at all, against the softest possible target. The council wins it. Nothing about how it evaluates hands changed; the exploit agent simply gets louder in proportion to how extreme the read is, and the lenses that can only see showdown value stand aside instead of voting the bluff down. That single rule is the difference between noticing you are being handed the pot and not.

    The equity bot. Against the engine's own simpler agent the council is level, not ahead — both rows sit inside their intervals. It reasons better, it shows its working, and it is roughly forty times slower per decision. On this evidence that trade has not yet bought anything against a competent opponent, and the table would be worth less if it pretended otherwise.

    Both numbers moved a long way during development, and not because of tuning. Two ordinary bugs were costing most of it: the playing agent was adding the bet it faced on top of a pot that already contained it, which quietly improved every price it was offered, and it was sampling its action from the vote share. Fixing the second alone was worth about 250 bb/100.

    What is claimed, and what is not

    Solved

    The jam/fold equilibrium and everything derived from it. Exact to the limits of floating point, measured by its own exploitability. If this page and a published Nash chart disagree, check the chart.

    Computed

    Hand evaluation, pot odds, board texture, blocker counts, range shape, equity. Enumerated exactly where the tree is small enough and sampled with a stated confidence interval where it is not.

    Measured

    The benchmark. Real hands, real variance, intervals attached. A result inside its interval is not a result, and the table above says so where that happens.

    Judged

    Every authority weight, every confidence curve, the sizing rules, the five opponent profiles. These are engineering choices, tuned against the tests and the benchmark. They are the part most likely to be wrong.

    Three things this is not

    It is not a solver. Postflop Hold'em has not been solved and this does not solve it. Outside the short-stack preflop game, the council estimates — carefully, from real numbers, with the error bars shown, but it estimates.

    It is not a tool for playing against people who think they are playing a human. It runs on hands you type into it, and it is built to be argued with rather than obeyed. Pointing it at a live table would break the terms of every real-money site there is, and take money from opponents who did not agree to play a machine. That is the one thing the engine deliberately cannot do.

    It is only as good as the range you give it. Every number on this page is conditional on the opponent's range, and the field marked "the range they would take this line with" means exactly that — not the hands they play, the hands they would bet here. Widen it and the adversary agent will tell you, in the panel at the top, whether the answer survives.
